August had a clear shape. It opened with the launch: the new site replaced the old one in the first week, with all 184 pages opened to Google at the moment of the switch, the sitemap submitted, and your email and booking subdomain protected through the domain change. The risk in replacing a site is losing the search visibility built up on the old one, so most of that week went on making the change invisible to Google rather than on anything you can see. The second week was settling in — the blog section, the landing pages for your ad campaigns, the first email and SMS campaigns out to dormant and existing customers, and a run of small corrections to images and the mobile booking bar of the kind that only surface once real visitors are on a new site. The third week turned to measurement and groundwork: a week-long post-launch watch for broken links and lost bookings, a full quality pass across all 31 email templates and 11 automated flows, the flash sale built as a split test across two audience groups, both Google Business Profiles updated, and a diagnosis of why general, non-brand search terms had been sliding. The last week was the heaviest and the most strategic: eight location pages optimised, four Near Me pages published, and the tracking work that lets us tell which spend produces which job — call tracking, booking attribution, and a connection that feeds completed bookings back into Google Ads as conversions. That work is invisible on the site and it is what makes the ad spending decisions defensible. The month ahead is intended to push further on location content, shift ad weighting toward mobile window tint, and establish a cost per booked job across Yelp and Google Ads. That is our sequencing rather than a set of promised dates.

August was the month the ad account scaled, and it scaled efficiently. Spend rose sharply and conversions rose slightly faster, so the extra budget bought more than it cost and the price of each conversion came down rather than up. That is the result that justifies spending more; the usual outcome when you push for volume is the opposite. Clicks fell over the same period, which sounds contradictory but is the mechanism — the account is buying fewer, better-matched clicks instead of more of everything. Two things to hold in mind when reading the conversion figure. Ads tracking was reworked during the month: a tracking fault was resolved, booking attribution was rebuilt, and completed bookings now feed back into Google Ads as conversions where previously they did not. That work makes the number more accurate going forward, but it means part of the increase is conversions we can now see rather than conversions that did not exist in July. We would rather say that than let you read the whole gain as performance. September is the first month measured the same way at both ends, and it is the one to judge on. Separately, these are account-level totals — the current feed carries no campaign breakdown, so which campaign drove the gain is not yet something we can show you.

Fewer visits, better visits. Sessions and users both came down against July, while the share of people leaving without going further improved and recorded conversions rose. That combination is the one worth having: the site is turning a smaller audience into more enquiries and bookings than the larger audience produced the month before. Some of the drop in raw visits is the site change itself, and one contributor is measurement rather than behaviour — how Yelp visits are categorised in analytics was found to be misattributed and was corrected during the month, which affects where traffic is credited. Judge the trend from next month, on a full period of the new site.

Search came through the rebuild intact, which is the main thing to establish in a launch month. Impressions grew, clicks edged up, and average position improved slightly — the site is being shown more often and ranked marginally better than it was on the old build. Click-through rate eased back, which is the expected consequence of appearing for a wider set of searches: more of the people seeing you are earlier in their decision. Underneath the totals, the newly optimised location pages are where the movement is — San Marcos and Oceanside both gained, and San Marcos in particular went from almost no visibility to a meaningful position. Against that, searches for your brand name shifted between variations rather than growing overall, and a few general, non-brand terms lost ground. That decline was investigated during the month and is what the location page programme and the Near Me pages are built to answer.
